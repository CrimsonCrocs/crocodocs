# Voice and article shape

Read this reference whenever `$studyvault-article-writer` is used.

## The intended reading experience

A StudyVault article should feel like a knowledgeable teacher has prepared one complete set of revision notes for a particular topic. It may sit inside a syllabus sequence, but it must not read like a numbered chapter whose opening depends on the previous page or whose ending advertises the next one.

The voice is clear, calm, direct, and alive. It should not sound like copied source notes, a generic chatbot, a motivational study guide, or a bureaucratic textbook. Precision matters more than flourish, but removing flourish must not flatten the prose into clipped or joyless sentences.

## Openings

Name and explain the central idea immediately in one or two short sentences, usually within about 45 words. A brief concrete contrast can help, but an extended story must not delay the explanation. Caribbean applications belong beside the concepts they clarify; they are not mandatory opening scenes.

For example: **Scarcity** means resources are limited compared with people's wants. It forces people, businesses and governments to choose how to use those resources.

Do not follow this with another paragraph announcing the article's scope. Begin the first useful section.

Avoid openings such as:

- A generic subject definition before an unrelated topic. A direct subject definition is appropriate when the article itself introduces that subject.
- "In this article, we will explore..."
- "This page covers..."
- "Having examined X, we now turn to Y."
- "As you learned in the previous article..."

A student may arrive from search, a shared link, or a syllabus index. The opening must work in all three cases.

## Paragraphs

A new paragraph marks a real movement: a new claim, stage, contrast, example, consequence, or perspective. It is not a visual spacer.

Keep connected reasoning, but do not merge paragraphs merely because they concern the same term. A definition, a mechanism and an example can be separate teaching steps. A paragraph that carries several such steps should be shortened or divided at a meaningful boundary. Avoid one-sentence stacks and dense walls equally.

Judge the break by meaning:

- same idea continuing: keep one paragraph;
- new idea or argumentative move: start another paragraph;
- list of genuinely parallel items: use a list or table;
- one isolated reminder: keep it inline unless an admonition materially improves recall.

Avoid mechanically uniform paragraph lengths. Rhythm should change because the reasoning changes.

## Scanning and vocabulary

Use the same plain surrounding language for CSEC and CAPE. Advanced ideas still need exact terms, assumptions, derivations and limitations; they do not need unnecessarily formal phrasing. Prefer "uses" to "utilises" and "shows" to "provides a graphical representation of" where meaning is unchanged.

Bold a newly defined term or a crucial condition sparingly. A student scanning headings and emphasis should find the structure of the explanation. Do not decorate every sentence or convert all prose to bullets. Use lists for genuine sets or steps and tables for useful comparisons.

Keep titles and descriptions recognisable and concise. Preserve subject meaning, slugs, syllabus mappings, equations, evidence and useful worked examples. Cut repeated exposition and generic claims of importance, not the reasoning a student needs.

## Standalone structure

Each article should provide enough local context to explain its own terms, methods, diagrams, and applications. Cross-links may enrich the page but must not be required to understand it.

Do not use continuity filler such as "next", "previously", "building on the last topic", or "we now move" unless it describes steps inside the current article. Topic pages should be independently useful even when the browse order changes.

Do not solve weak continuity by splitting the article into more pages. First repair the opening, definitions, transitions, and local context. Split only when the topic itself exceeds a coherent revision session or the user requests a new boundary.

## Explanations and examples

- Move from the concrete problem to the term or rule, then to the mechanism and consequence.
- State the trigger for a method explicitly. Do not assume that seeing an example teaches when to use it.
- Use plain language around exact technical vocabulary. Repeat the correct term instead of cycling through decorative synonyms.
- Prefer examples that require the same reasoning as an examination question. Remove examples that merely restate the sentence above them.
- Apply recommendations to facts in the case. "A bank transfer is convenient" is weak; explain which need it satisfies and why.
- Distinguish similar terms where confusion is likely.

## Deslop without deadening the prose

Run `$deslop` as a preservation pass, not a tone replacement.

Remove clusters of:

- throat-clearing and article announcements;
- generic significance claims;
- repeated mini-summaries;
- formulaic three-part paragraphs;
- metronomic sentence and paragraph shapes;
- fake enthusiasm, decorative metaphors, and generic endings;
- excessive instruction directed at the student;
- transitions that exist only to make separate pages sound continuous.

Keep:

- necessary technical vocabulary;
- useful Caribbean or exam-relevant context;
- a natural mix of short and developed sentences;
- occasional warmth, concrete imagery, and well-earned emphasis;
- uncertainty and limitations supported by the source.

If a Deslop edit makes the explanation clunky, overly compressed, impersonal, or harder for a student to follow, reject that edit.

## Assets and source integrity

StudyVault must not publish diagrams lifted from copyrighted textbooks or revision guides. Internal copyrighted material can reveal what must be taught, but its artwork and phrasing are not reusable assets.

For a needed visual, use this order:

1. an original diagram built with the project's supported tools;
2. a clearly licensed open or public-domain asset with recorded provenance;
3. a specific placeholder when neither is currently possible.

Do not closely redraw a copyrighted figure merely to change its file format or visual style.

## Final read

Read the rendered article once as a student who landed on it directly. Check:

- Does the first paragraph establish the topic without administrative framing?
- Does any sentence assume another article has already been read?
- Do paragraph breaks correspond to changes in thought?
- Does every heading earn its space?
- Does each component teach something prose alone would not present as well?
- Is the last paragraph useful rather than ceremonial?
- Does the prose still sound like a person teaching, not a rules engine editing?
