Learn to design parts and mechanisms for an FTC robot, even if you have never opened CAD. **CAD**, short for computer-aided design, lets you make accurate models, check how parts fit, and prepare the files used to make them.

The handbook starts with measurement, simple sketches, and a plate assembly. It then works through moving joints, transmissions, arms, lifts, intakes, wiring space, and manufacturing. You can follow the learning route or open a chapter when your team reaches that job.

Keep a notebook beside the model. Record why you chose a dimension, what you measured, and what changed after a test. Those details help the next student understand the design instead of merely copying its shape.

## Start here

If you have never used CAD, work through these five guides in order. You will make one mounting plate, add named dimensions, and assemble two copies with two spacers. Keep the same document throughout.

1. [Onshape](/docs/design/onshape): accounts, documents, units, and finding your way around.
2. [Sketches](/docs/design/sketches): dimensions and constraints that keep a design predictable.
3. [Parts and variables](/docs/design/parts-and-variables): turn a sketch into a part you can change.
4. [Assemblies](/docs/design/assemblies): build a plate-and-spacer assembly and check its dimensions.
5. [Drawings and exports](/docs/design/drawings-and-exports): make a drawing, export the plate, and list the assembly’s parts.

If a term interrupts you, use the [Design glossary](/docs/design/glossary). For the wider job of starting an FTC team, use the [Starter Guide](/docs/starter-guide).

## Before you design

You can practice CAD before you have a robot kit. For a real mechanism, begin with the task and the hardware you will use.

| Chapter | What you will learn |
| --- | --- |
| [Your first design brief](/docs/design/design-brief) | Describe the task, compare concepts, and choose a useful prototype |
| [Measuring parts](/docs/design/measuring-parts) | Read drawings, find hole centers, and record mounting interfaces |
| [Forces and motion](/docs/design/forces-and-motion) | Estimate torque, speed, loads, and the effect of a reduction |

The examples use millimeters for CAD and meters where the physics equations require them. They identify their practice dimensions; replace those with the selected hardware and current game requirements when designing your robot.

## Learn more Onshape

After the first plate, make a bracket, a moving hinge, and a family of spacers. These exercises introduce tools you will use repeatedly on the robot.

| Chapter | What you will make or check |
| --- | --- |
| [More sketch tools](/docs/design/sketch-tools) | An adjustment slot and a rounded link |
| [Modeling features](/docs/design/modeling-features) | An L-bracket, repeated cuts, and a revolved spacer |
| [Curved and hollow parts](/docs/design/curved-and-hollow-parts) | A funnel using offset planes, Loft, and Shell |
| [Moving assemblies](/docs/design/moving-assemblies) | A hinge with a defined axis, offset, and travel limits |
| [Hardware and imports](/docs/design/hardware-and-imports) | Correct purchased-part references and simple envelopes |
| [Configurations](/docs/design/configurations) | Three spacer lengths controlled by one model |
| [Custom features](/docs/design/custom-features) | Reusable FeatureScript tools and controlled updates |

Stay with the standard tools until the operation makes sense. A custom feature can save drawing work, but you still need to understand the part it creates.

Once you can make and assemble simple parts, work out how the whole robot fits together.

Start with simple shapes. A rectangle reserving room for the battery is useful long before you model its latch. Add detail when it answers a fit, motion, strength, or manufacturing question.

## Review and practice

Use [CAD teamwork](/docs/design/cad-teamwork) to share references and keep files matched to the robot. Before making a batch, run a [Design review](/docs/design/design-review) with the builders and programmers. Use [CAD troubleshooting](/docs/design/cad-troubleshooting) when a sketch, feature, mate, or export stops behaving correctly.

The [Practice project](/docs/design/practice-project) brings the lessons together in a belt-driven bench roller module. Make the layout, parts, moving assembly, drawing, and BOM, then ask another student to review it.

The [CAD examples](/docs/design/cad-examples) study specific choices by Pretty Smart Robotics 16441, Hyperion 9614, and Seattle Solvers 23511, with credited images you can open at full size. Use their decisions as questions to investigate in your own design, rather than treating one team's geometry as a universal template.

The [Engineering handbook](/docs/engineering) covers the mechanics behind these designs, including motors, gears, materials, wiring, and testing. Use both sections together: CAD can show that parts fit, while a physical test tells you whether the mechanism works.
