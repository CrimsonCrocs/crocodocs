Design the support and the drive connection separately. Bearings carry forces while allowing rotation; a hub, key, shaped bore, or clamping connection carries torque. A shaft drawn through a hole still needs both jobs accounted for.

Use the [Engineering guide to shafts and bearings](/docs/engineering/mechanical/shafts-and-bearings) for component types. This page applies them to a CAD assembly.

## Put supports near the load

Draw the shaft's centerline and the points where external loads act. Then place the supports. A roller between two bearings usually loads the structure differently from a roller hanging beyond one bearing.

For a simplified shaft with supports **80 mm apart** and a **20 N downward force halfway between them**, each support provides 10 N upward. If the same load acts **20 mm outside** the left support, the reactions become 25 N upward at the left and 5 N downward at the right.

The second case creates a larger load at the nearby support and needs the distant support to resist the opposite direction. Moving the roller outward is therefore a structural change, even though the shaft still fits both holes. Actual shafts also see transmission forces, impact, and deflection.

## Match the interfaces

A bearing has an **inner race** around the shaft and an **outer race** in the housing. A flange or shoulder can locate it axially. Check the real drawing for bore, body diameter, width, flange, and corner clearances.

Build a small interface table before making the plate:

| Interface | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Shaft to bearing | Bore size and profile, shaft tolerance, any step or shoulder |
| Bearing to plate | Housing diameter, fit, supporting thickness, retention |
| Shaft to hub | Round, hex, D, REX, keyed, or another actual torque interface |
| Hub to mechanism | Bolt pattern, clamping length, screw access |
| Axial stack | Spacer lengths, collars, shoulders, washers, and end screws |

A bearing for an 8 mm round shaft will not automatically support every nominally 8 mm shaped shaft. Likewise, a stepped shaft can use different bearing bores at different locations. Follow the supplier's assembly rather than replacing its dimensions with one assumed diameter.

## Model a support plate

For a geometry exercise, sketch a **60 × 50 mm** plate on Front, with the lower-edge midpoint at the origin. Place a **16 mm opening at (0, 25) mm**, and two **4.5 mm mounting holes at (−20, 8) and (20, 8) mm**. Extrude the region **3 mm**.

The 16 mm opening is a nominal practice seat. Before manufacturing a bearing housing, choose the actual bearing and a fit suited to the material and process. A nominally equal CAD diameter does not specify a press fit or guarantee a printed fit.

Insert two plate instances into an Assembly, parallel, with **80 mm between their inner faces**. Use standoffs or other structural references to define that gap. Put the bearing axes on a common line and add the chosen bearings.

Inspect a section through the shaft. Check which surface stops each bearing from moving in either axial direction. A flange stops movement in one direction; the rest of the mounting arrangement must handle the other direction where needed.

## Lay out the complete stack

Work along the shaft from one end to the other. Include every component that occupies length, including collars, bearing projections, hub shoulders, pulley flanges, and washers.

For an illustrative **80 mm clear space**, suppose bearing flanges occupy 1 mm at each inner face. That leaves **78 mm**. A 50 mm roller/hub envelope, a 12 mm pulley/hub envelope, and 14 mm of spacers use 76 mm, leaving 2 mm for resolving the final arrangement.

That 2 mm is unallocated space. It is not a requirement for 2 mm of shaft movement. Choose the final spacers and retention so the shaft stays located without binding the bearings.

Model the full outside length too. A pulley or collar outside the plate can collide with a wheel, field wall, or another mechanism. The shaft must be long enough for its required engagement and retention, without leaving an unnecessary exposed end.

## Tighten the intended surfaces

For a live shaft, collars and spacers commonly contact the rotating inner races. A spacer with too large an outside diameter can rub a stationary outer race, seal, or housing. Inspect the contact in section rather than judging from an outside view.

For a dead axle, the axle stays fixed and the mechanism rotates around it. The inner-race spacing must let the axle be tightened without squeezing the rotating outer assembly. The torque path belongs to the rotating wheel or pulley, not to the stationary axle.

Use suitable hardware and the manufacturer's instructions. Tightening harder is not a repair for a stack that clamps the wrong parts together.

## Align, then test

Locate both support openings from shared geometry. If you dimension them independently from different edges, small changes can make their axes disagree.

Before attaching the motor or transmission, assemble the supports and turn the shaft by hand with power disconnected. Tighten the structure progressively and check whether resistance appears. Then add the hub, retention, and transmission in stages.

A tight spot once per revolution suggests a different problem from a shaft that binds only after the plates are tightened. Inspect runout, bent hardware, support alignment, and the contact stack. Record the final spacer arrangement in CAD before ordering replacement parts.
