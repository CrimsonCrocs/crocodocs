A transfer needs a repeatable **pose**: both the object's position and its orientation. Reaching the receiver's center is not enough if the object arrives turned the wrong way.

This example uses a simple pivoting arm and a rectangular practice object. Use a separate Onshape document called `Transfer layout practice`, with millimeter units. The [engineering design process](/docs/engineering/design/design-process) covers choosing requirements and prototypes; this page works through the geometry.

## Define the task

In a side-view sketch, use horizontal distance for forward position and vertical distance for height. The example has:

| Quantity | Practice value |
| --- | --- |
| Arm pivot | (80, 100) mm |
| Pivot to object center | 120 mm |
| Intake position | 30° below horizontal |
| Handoff position | 60° above horizontal |
| Object | 40 × 20 mm rectangle, with its long side parallel to the arm |
| Receiver opening | 44 × 24 mm, aligned with the handoff object |

These describe a geometry exercise, not a complete claw or a season's game piece. The arm must move through **90°** between the two positions.

## Draw the two poses [#compare-simple-layouts]

1. Start a sketch on the Front plane. Draw construction lines through the origin for the floor and horizontal reference.
2. Locate the pivot 80 mm forward and 100 mm above the origin.
3. Draw a 120 mm construction line from the pivot, angled **30° below horizontal**. Its endpoint is the intake object center.
4. Draw a second 120 mm construction line from the same pivot, angled **60° above horizontal**. Its endpoint is the handoff center.
5. At each endpoint, draw a centered 40 × 20 mm rectangle. Constrain its long sides parallel to that position's arm line.
6. Add a centered 44 × 24 mm receiver rectangle at the handoff, with the same orientation.

Measure the endpoints to check your sketch:

| Pose | Forward position | Height | Object angle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intake | about 183.9 mm | 40.0 mm | −30° |
| Handoff | 140.0 mm | about 203.9 mm | 60° |

The coordinates come from `pivot + arm length × (cos(angle), sin(angle))`, with the calculator in degrees. Cosine gives the horizontal part of the arm's length; sine gives the vertical part.

If the receiver required a **horizontal** object instead, the same arm motion would not satisfy that pose. You would need another arrangement: reorient the object earlier, add a wrist, or choose motion that preserves its angle. [Hyperion's example](/docs/design/cad-examples#hyperion) shows how an FTC team used transfer orientation to simplify its outtake.

## Check capture clearance [#prototype-the-uncertain-part]

The 44 × 24 mm opening gives the 40 × 20 mm object **2 mm per side** at the nominal pose. Now test a 3 mm offset perpendicular to its long side while keeping the object angle fixed. One edge extends **1 mm beyond** the opening.

That tells you a 2 mm allowance cannot accept that particular offset. Widening the opening may help, but also check whether the guides can still retain the object and whether the claw's fingers fit. Rotation, flexible contact, and three-dimensional interference need their own checks.

Build an adjustable receiver and present the object from the actual intake, including imperfect positions. Record successful captures and misses before choosing the final guide spacing. Use [Transfers](/docs/engineering/mechanisms/transfers) for contact, sensing, and recovery.

## Agree on interfaces

Record the result so the other mechanism designer and programmer use the same reference:

| Interface | What to communicate |
| --- | --- |
| Arm mounting | Pivot location and axis, measured from the robot datums |
| Handoff | Object center (140, 203.9) mm and 60° orientation in this exercise |
| Receiver | Opening, allowed offset, retention surfaces, and finger clearance |
| Sequence | Receiver captures before donor releases, where the geometry allows it |
| Recovery | A return path that clears both mechanisms after a miss |

A servo command or a closed-claw drawing does not establish capture. Decide what physical evidence or tested timing permits the next movement.

## Review before detailing

Sweep the object and arm between the two poses. Add the neighboring frame, intake, receiver, and cable space to the sketch or assembly. Check intermediate positions and the return movement, then make the uncertain contact geometry as a small prototype.

Only after that should you detail the plates, joints, and actuator mount. Use [Arms and linkages](/docs/engineering/mechanisms/arms-and-linkages) to size the load and support; the pose sketch establishes reach and orientation, not motor capacity.
