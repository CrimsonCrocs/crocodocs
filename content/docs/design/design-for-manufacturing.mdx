A part that is easy to draw may be difficult to make. **Design for manufacturing** means choosing geometry with the actual tools, material, and assembly process in mind.

Choose the process before finishing the model. A flat plate cut from sheet, a printed bracket, and a milled block each need different details.

## Use stock sizes

Check what your team can buy before choosing thicknesses, shaft lengths, or spacers. A 3 mm sheet and a 1/8 inch sheet are not the same thickness. That difference can matter in an axle stack or a tightly fitted assembly.

Model the stock you will actually use. Confirm material and thickness with the supplier, and measure critical stock when it arrives. If a part needs several machining operations to replace a readily available spacer, compare the effort with buying the spacer.

## Flat plates

Flat plates work well when their important features can be cut from sheet and assembled with ordinary hardware.

Keep access holes distinct from mounting holes. Check screw-head and nut clearance, distance from holes to edges, bearing retention, and the structure joining loaded areas. A thin strip around a large opening may flex even though the CAD model looks substantial.

With a rotating cutting tool, internal corners cannot be perfectly square. Choose a corner radius the available tool can make, or provide suitable relief where a rectangular part must fit. Ask the manufacturer what its process requires before changing the geometry.

## Weight-saving cutouts

**Pocketing** removes material to reduce mass. A cut all the way through a plate is different from a shallow milled pocket; make sure your process can produce what the model shows.

Start by identifying the load path: where forces enter, where they leave, and which material carries them. Preserve support around bearings, fasteners, and crossmember mounts. Large openings can reduce stiffness and make a plate easier to twist.

Decorative patterns are not a structural calculation. A pattern that works on another team's plate may be unsuitable for your bearing locations, material, thickness, and loads. Compare mass and measured deflection before deciding whether a cutout is useful.

## Printed parts

Orient a print with the loads and layer directions in mind. Avoid placing a major separating load across a weak layer interface. Include space for fasteners and a practical way to install nuts or inserts.

Use small test pieces for uncertain fits. A bearing pocket, shaft opening, and screw hole need not all use the same allowance. Check printer, material, orientation, and settings before applying a result to the final part.

Allow adjustment where the mechanism needs tuning. Slotted roller mounts or replaceable spacers can make compression tests easier, provided the final joint can be locked securely.

## Check the assembly

For each custom part, answer four questions:

1. How is it made, and which dimensions matter most?
2. How is it held and measured during manufacturing?
3. How does it go into the robot, including its fasteners?
4. How is it removed when damaged?

Then build one representative joint or fit coupon. Correct the model using measured evidence before making the whole set.
