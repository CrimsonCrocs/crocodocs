Before drawing a mechanism, write down what it must do. A **design brief** is a short description of the task, its limits, and how you will check the result. One page is enough for a small FTC mechanism.

Suppose your team needs a roller to move a game piece from the intake into a claw. “Make a fast transfer” leaves a lot unanswered. Where does the game piece start? Which way should it face when the claw grabs it? What happens if it comes out of the intake crooked? How much room do you have?

Write those answers down so everyone is designing for the same job. They also give you a way to compare ideas before spending time on detailed CAD.

## Plan the handoff [#describe-the-object-and-movement]

Start with the actual game piece, intake, and claw. Measure several game pieces if you can. Check their size, where they flex, and whether seams or other features affect how they roll or grip. Try feeding them through the intake in different ways and see how they come out.

Draw a side view and a top view. Show the game piece in the intake, as it moves toward the claw, and inside the closed claw. At each step, show where it sits and which way it faces. Reaching the claw is only part of the job: the game piece also needs to be at an angle the claw can grab. In robotics, **pose** means position and orientation together.

Use these questions to describe the handoff:

- How does the game piece come out of the intake? Could it be sideways or off to one side?
- Where does it need to be for the claw to grab it? Does it need to face a certain way?
- What holds it up while it moves from the intake to the claw?
- If the handoff fails, what can the driver do to try again?

Add sketches, dimensions, and photographs where they help. For example, draw arrows on a photo of the intake to show where the game piece can sit.

## Requirements and preferences

A **requirement** is something the design must satisfy. A **preference** helps rank designs that satisfy the requirements. A **constraint** limits the available choices, such as a fixed mounting face, budget, or manufacturing process.

For a transfer you are testing on the bench, your notes might look like this:

| Item | Example | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting point | Game piece rests in the intake guide | Put it in different positions and try the handoff |
| At the claw | Game piece reaches the place and angle shown in the claw drawing | Check the dimensions and watch whether the claw grabs it |
| Space | The transfer fits in the room left for it | Move it through its full travel in the robot assembly |
| Timing | Finish within the time the team has allowed for the handoff | Time the whole attempt, including any retry |
| Construction | Make custom parts with the team's printer and drill press | Review every feature with the people making it |
| Preference | Less weight for the motor to move | Compare the weight of designs that can do the job |

Choose timing and success targets that suit your robot. Say how you will measure them. For a reliability test, record how you place the game piece and what counts as a successful handoff, so everyone tests the same thing.

Check the current game manual for rules that affect the design, including size, extension, materials, motors or servos, and game-piece handling. Keep the relevant rule references with your notes so the team can find them again.

## Compare different mechanisms

Sketch two or three ideas before choosing one to model in detail. You could try a claw that swings the game piece over, rollers that push it along a guide, or a claw that moves toward the intake to pick it up.

Think through the whole handoff for each idea. Rollers need guides, supports, a connection to the motor, and somewhere to hold the game piece until the claw is ready. Claws need room to open, and the code has to open and close them in the right order. Include what the programmers must write and what the driver must do.

You can use a small decision table:

| Question | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Can it pick up and deliver the game piece? | A sketch with dimensions showing both positions and which way the game piece faces |
| What is uncertain? | The contact, stiffness, or movement you have not tested |
| Can the team build it? | Stock, tools, purchased components, and difficult operations |
| Can it be repaired? | Removal steps and parts needed for a replacement |
| What would make it fail? | A specific problem, such as dropping the game piece while turning |

If an idea cannot get the game piece into the claw, change it or set it aside. A low cost or weight does not make up for being unable to do the job.

## Prototype the uncertain part

A **prototype** answers a question before you commit to the full design. It can be cardboard, a printed guide, or two rollers on adjustable mounts.

If the uncertainty is ball contact, test the roller spacing and guide shape. You do not need a finished chassis for that experiment. If the uncertainty is access to a mounting screw, a full-size cardboard mock-up may be enough.

Write the question before making the prototype. Then record what changed between attempts, the starting conditions, and what happened. Keep failed attempts: they often explain why the final geometry has a guide, a larger opening, or a different pivot.

## Before you start CAD [#give-the-designer-a-starting-package]

Before you start detailed CAD, put your notes, measurements, purchased-part models, layout sketch, and prototype results in one place. Include the mounting-hole positions and the surfaces the new part will attach to. Make clear which dimensions are fixed and which can change.

For example, you cannot move the holes in a rail you already own, but you may be able to change the gap between two custom side plates. Agree on that before teammates start designing parts around them.

Use [Measuring parts](/docs/design/measuring-parts) to collect dimensions, then [Layout sketches](/docs/design/layout-sketches) to turn the brief into shared geometry. If CAD is new to you, start the [Onshape lessons](/docs/design/onshape) alongside this work.
