A custom drivetrain can free space for an intake or place motors where a kit chassis cannot. It also makes your team responsible for axle alignment, shaft support, tensioning, and replacement parts.

Keep a kit drivetrain when it meets your needs. Choose custom plates to solve a specific problem you can describe and test.

## Start with the wheels

Choose the wheel type, diameter, wheelbase, and track width. **Wheelbase** is the front-to-back distance between axle centers. **Track width** is the side-to-side distance between wheel centers.

Draw the wheels and floor in the layout. Include the real width of the wheel, its hub, and the space needed by nearby plates. Check ground clearance with the expected load and field surface.

Mecanum wheels must be oriented correctly, and their rollers need room to rotate. Use [Drivetrains](/docs/engineering/mechanisms/drivetrains) for wheel arrangement and drive behavior.

## Live and dead axles

| Arrangement | What rotates | Main design checks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live axle | The shaft turns with the wheel and driven pulley or sprocket | Bearings support the shaft; hubs transmit torque; collars or clips retain it |
| Dead axle | The wheel assembly rotates around a stationary axle | Bearings are in the rotating assembly; the axle is secured; tightening does not clamp the rotating assembly solid |

Neither arrangement dictates which side of the wheel holds the transmission. Putting it outside the central robot space may help packaging, but it can increase width or make the transmission more exposed.

For a live-axle wheel module, identify every interface before drawing its side plates:

| Component | What it must match |
| --- | --- |
| Wheel and hub | Wheel bolt pattern, hub bore, and torque connection |
| Shaft | Hub bore, bearing bores, and retention hardware |
| Two bearings | Shaft diameter, plate seats, and flange/shoulder locations |
| Pulley or sprocket and hub | Shaft connection, belt/chain profile, width, and alignment |
| Spacers, shims, and collars | The races or shoulders they contact; clearance from rotating faces |

Make a section along the axle. For an illustrative **60 mm gap between inner plate faces**, suppose each bearing projects 2 mm into the gap. That leaves **56 mm** for the rotating assembly. A 28 mm wheel/hub envelope, a 12 mm pulley/hub envelope, and 14 mm of spacers use **54 mm**, leaving **2 mm unallocated**.

That remainder tells you the arrangement has room for adjustment; it is not a target for 2 mm of shaft endplay. Locate the shaft with suitable retention, choose the final spacer/shim stack, and check free rotation after tightening. A collar or spacer must bear on the intended rotating race without rubbing the fixed housing.

For a real hardware study, the goBILDA Strafer hub-shaft `2110-0608-0002` uses an **8 mm-ID bearing and a 6 mm-ID bearing** at different shaft locations, with shims and an end screw/washer. Its assembly instructions show why choosing bearings from one nominal shaft size can be misleading. Inspect that supplier stack before adapting its support geometry to a custom plate.

## Fit the transmission

The **transmission** carries rotation from the motor to the wheel, using gears, a belt, or a chain. **Pitch** describes the spacing of its teeth or links; matching pitch alone is not enough if their profiles differ.

Pick real gears, pulleys, belts, or sprockets before fixing their centers in the plate. Tooth pitch, tooth profile, width, and available lengths must match.

For compatible standard spur gears, the nominal center distance is:

$$
C = \frac{m(N_1+N_2)}{2}
$$

Here $m$ is module, the metric measure of tooth size, in millimeters. $N_1,N_2$ are tooth counts. Two illustrative module-0.8 gears with 20 and 40 teeth have a nominal center distance of 24 mm. Confirm the manufacturer's requirements, including backlash and any profile changes, before using that dimension.

For a timing pulley, pitch diameter is $D_p = Np/\pi$, where $p$ is belt pitch. A layout circle should represent this pitch diameter, not automatically the outside of the teeth or flange. Matching circles does not prove that a belt of the required length exists.

Plan tension adjustment and tool access. A belt that can only be installed by bending the frame is an assembly problem. Excessive tension can overload bearings and bend plates.

## Belt spacing

Use the side layout from [Layout sketches](/docs/design/layout-sketches). Its example transmission has a **20-tooth driver**, **40-tooth driven pulley**, and **2 mm belt pitch**. The reduction is 40/20 = **2:1**: the wheel shaft turns once for every two motor-output turns.

The pulley pitch diameters are approximately **12.73 mm** and **25.46 mm**. A 220 mm pitch-length belt has **220 / 2 = 110 teeth**. With this belt, the two-pulley geometry gives a center distance of about **79.75 mm**. A pitch length measures around the belt's working pitch line, not its outside surface.

Use a calculator for the selected belt profile and pulley sizes, then confirm the manufacturer's installation and tension requirements. Include adjustment without shifting the wheel axes out of alignment. A nominal 80 mm center distance is close to this example, but it is not the same constraint as the chosen belt length.

In the practice layout, the front wheel axis is 52 mm above the floor, so the motor axis goes at **52 + 79.75 = 131.75 mm**. Moving the motor changes the belt route and the available chassis space; it should update a shared layout, not an isolated mounting hole.

## Design the plates

Locate axle centers and motor mounts from the shared layout. Add crossmembers or standoffs that keep the two plates parallel under load.

Leave material around bearing seats and attachment points. Draw the likely force path from wheel contact through the axle supports into the frame before adding weight-saving cutouts. Read [Design for manufacturing](/docs/design/design-for-manufacturing) before pocketing the plate.

## Prototype one side

Check one drive module before cutting the whole chassis. Verify that it assembles, rotates freely, tensions correctly, and can be serviced. Then test straight driving, turning, braking, and simultaneous mechanism use on the complete robot.

The [Pretty Smart Robotics example](/docs/design/cad-examples#pretty-smart-robotics) shows how one team separates the side plates, motor assembly, and wheel assemblies into manageable design tasks.
