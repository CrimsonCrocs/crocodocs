An **assembly** contains instances of parts. An instance is one use of a part: four identical wheels can come from one wheel model.

**Mates** define the allowed movement between instances. They should describe how the real hardware is connected.

## Insert parts

Create an Assembly tab and insert the [practice plate](/docs/design/parts-and-variables) from your document. Add a second plate or a purchased component with a matching mounting pattern.

For purchased hardware, use the FTC parts resources or the manufacturer's model for the exact part number. A similar-looking motor may have a different shaft length or mounting pattern. Check a known dimension after importing.

Keep purchased parts separate from custom parts. Remodeling a complete motor rarely helps a bracket design; including its shaft, case, mounting holes, and cable exit usually does.

## Establish a reference

Choose one base component to locate the assembly. You can fix it in a deliberate position or mate it to the assembly origin. Do this before connecting the other components, so the whole mechanism does not drift when dragged.

A **mate connector** is a reference point with directions attached. Onshape can infer one from geometry, such as the center of a circular edge, or you can define one explicitly. Its orientation matters: it determines the axes used by the mate.

## Choose a mate

| Mate | Allowed motion | Robot example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fastened | None between the two parts | Sensor attached to its mount |
| Revolute | Rotation about one axis | Arm rotating around a supported shaft |
| Slider | Movement along one axis | Slide carriage moving along its rail |
| Cylindrical | Rotation and sliding along one axis | A shaft that genuinely has both motions |

For the practice assembly, fasten the second part to the plate using a suitable mounting reference. Check the offset and connector directions rather than dragging it until the surfaces appear aligned.

A revolute mate is not a bearing design. It permits motion in CAD even if the physical shaft has no support. Include the bearings, shaft retention, and mounting hardware that make that motion possible.

## Check a moving mechanism

Drag or animate the intended joint. Add limits matching the usable physical range. Inspect intermediate positions as well as the two endpoints.

Do not assume that a CAD animation stops at every collision. Use interference checks and visual inspection at the important positions. Flexible wires, belt tension, bearing fits, and structural deflection also need separate checks.

If a mechanism has been made rigid as a subassembly, check whether its intended internal motion is available in the parent assembly. A rigid representation can be useful for packaging, but it cannot demonstrate the full movement.

## Include the small hardware

Finish one representative joint completely: screw head, washers, spacer, bearings, collar or other retention, and nut. Then inspect a section through the shaft.

Ask how the hardware is assembled. Can the shaft slide into place? Can a wrench reach the nut? Does tightening the fastener clamp a bearing correctly, or accidentally squeeze the rotating part?

Use [Shafts and bearings](/docs/engineering/mechanical/shafts-and-bearings) for the physical details and [Robot packaging](/docs/design/packaging) for access and motion clearance.
