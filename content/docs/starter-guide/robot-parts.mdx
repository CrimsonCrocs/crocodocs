An FTC robot combines a structure, moving parts, electronics, and code. Learning the names makes it easier to follow kit instructions and ask for help.

## The structure and drive base

The **chassis** is the robot's main frame. It holds parts in place and carries loads. A **drive base**, or **drivetrain**, is the arrangement of motors, wheels, and supporting parts that moves the robot across the floor.

**Tank drive** uses wheels on the left and right sides. Running the sides at different speeds turns the robot. **Mecanum wheels** have angled rollers; with the right layout and code, four of them let the robot move sideways as well as forward and backward. That sideways movement is called **strafing**.

Tank drive is a useful place to learn. Mecanum can give drivers more movement options, but wheel placement, weight distribution, and code must be correct. Start with a documented kit layout your team can support.

A **fastener** joins parts together. Common examples are screws, nuts, and washers. A **standoff** is a spacer that holds two parts apart. Tighten parts securely without crushing plastic or damaging threads, the spiral ridges that let a screw engage with a nut.

## Motors and servos

A **motor** turns electrical energy into rotation. FTC drive motors usually have a **gearbox**, which uses gears to reduce speed and increase turning force at the output. That turning force is called **torque**.

**RPM** means revolutions per minute: how many full turns a shaft makes in one minute. A faster motor is not automatically better. A heavy lift may need more torque and a slower output. The [motor and gear guide](/docs/engineering/mechanical/motors-and-gearing) explains this in more detail.

A **servo** is a different kind of actuator. An **actuator** is a device that makes something move. A positional servo moves its output toward a commanded position; it is useful for a small latch or claw. A continuous-rotation servo is commanded by speed and direction instead, so check the model and operating mode you actually have.

## Gears, belts, and bearings

**Gears** are toothed wheels that pass rotation between shafts. A **belt** passes motion between pulleys. **Chain** passes motion between toothed sprockets. These let you put a motor away from the part it drives, change speed and torque, or drive several parts together.

A **bearing** supports a shaft while letting it rotate. A shaft with poor support can bend or bind. **Binding** means unwanted friction or misalignment prevents free movement.

A **mechanism** is a set of parts arranged to perform a task. An intake collects objects. A lift raises something. A claw holds an object. Learn one mechanism at a time and test it with the real object.

## Controllers and sensors

The **Robot Controller** runs your robot program. A REV **Control Hub** combines that computer with ports for motors, servos, and sensors. An optional **Expansion Hub** adds more device connections; it does not replace the computer running the program.

The **Driver Station** is the device students use to select a program, start and stop it, and send gamepad input. A REV Driver Hub is one type of Driver Station device.

A **sensor** measures something. An **encoder** measures rotation, a distance sensor measures distance, and an **IMU** measures motion and orientation. IMU stands for *inertial measurement unit*. Sensors give your code information, but the code must decide what to do with it.

Point out these parts on your kit robot. If you cannot find one, check whether your kit actually includes it before assuming something is missing.
