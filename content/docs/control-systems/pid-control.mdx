# Make an arm stop at a chosen angle

Imagine your arm must lift to 60°. With a timed command, you might run its motor for half a second and hope it stops there. The arm can stop short when the battery is low, or travel too far when the arm is lighter. A **PID controller** checks the arm's encoder while it moves and adjusts motor power based on what the arm actually did.

This lesson builds that controller in stages. You will first check the encoder and motor direction, then add proportional control, and only then add derivative and integral correction. The example is for a single encoder-equipped arm; your gear ratio, arm range, motor direction, and gains must be measured on your robot.

![A feedback loop: compare the target angle to the measured angle, adjust motor power, and measure again.](/guides/pid-feedback-loop.svg)

## Before you start

You need:

- A motor with an encoder attached to the arm.
- The motor configured in the Robot Controller configuration. This example calls it `arm`.
- A known safe low and high arm position, with hard stops or another way to keep the arm inside its real travel range.
- A way to measure the arm angle, such as a printed angle guide or protractor.

**Make the mechanism safe before testing.** Support the arm so it cannot fall, keep hands clear of gears and pinch points, and begin with a small power limit. If motor power moves the arm away from the target, stop the OpMode and correct the motor direction before continuing.

## First, understand feedback

A timed motor command is open-loop: you choose a power and duration, then hope the arm ends up where you wanted. Battery voltage, friction, and the arm's load can change the result. A feedback controller closes that gap by measuring what happened and correcting it repeatedly:

1. Read the encoder and convert its counts to the arm's angle.
2. Compare that measured angle with the target.
3. Turn the difference into a motor-power command.
4. Apply the command briefly, then measure again on the next loop.

The target is the **setpoint**, the encoder-derived angle is the **measurement**, and their difference is the **error**:

```text
error = target angle - measured angle
```

If the target is 60° and the encoder says 20°, the error is `60 - 20 = 40°`. Positive error means the arm needs to move toward a larger angle. Your motor's configured direction determines which power sign produces that motion, so verify the sign at low power before tuning.

The letters describe three ways of responding to that error. A common continuous-time form is:

You can skip the equation on a first read. The table below gives the practical meaning of each part.

$$
u(t) = k_P e(t) + k_I \int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau + k_D \frac{de(t)}{dt}
$$

Here `e` is the error and `u` is the requested motor command. The controller below runs digitally, so it approximates the integral by adding `error × elapsed time` on each loop. It estimates speed from the change in measured angle divided by elapsed time. The gains are tied to these units: with angle in degrees and output in motor power, `kP` has units of power per degree; `kI` has units of power per degree-second; `kD` has units of power-seconds per degree. Changing the sensor units or time units means the gains must change too.

### What each term contributes

- **P — Proportional:** reacts to the gap right now. A bigger gap asks for a stronger push. Start here; too much P can make the arm overshoot.
- **I — Integral:** reacts to an error that keeps happening. It can help when the arm repeatedly stops a little short, but stored error can build up while the motor is blocked. Leave I at zero until P and D behave well.
- **D — Derivative:** reacts to how fast the arm is moving. It can brake an arm approaching its target, but too much D can make the motor chatter.

The finished example limits stored I error and estimates D from measured arm speed. These choices avoid two common problems, but the gains and limits still need tuning on your robot.

The example combines the terms like this:

```text
requested power = P contribution + I contribution + D contribution
               = kP × error + kI × accumulated error - kD × measured speed
```

The minus sign on the speed term makes it oppose the arm's current movement. The requested power is then limited to the configured maximum. Once that limit is reached, a bigger controller request cannot produce more motor power, which is why integral buildup needs a limit too.

PID does not mean all three gains must be nonzero. Start with P, add D if the arm overshoots, and try I only when repeatable tests show a small error that remains. An arm also has gravity acting on it; a PID loop may need a properly measured gravity-compensation term to hold a position without a large integral correction.

### A quick mental model

| Term | Question it answers | Arm intuition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P | How far away are we right now? | Push toward the target in proportion to the gap. |
| I | Has a small error kept happening? | Add a little more push to overcome a persistent load or friction. |
| D | How fast are we moving? | Ease off while moving quickly toward the target. |

For an FTC arm, begin with P only. Add D if it overshoots. Add I only if it consistently stops short after P and D are tuned. Integral is not automatically needed; an arm that must hold itself against gravity may need gravity feedforward or a carefully limited integral term.

## Step 1: Give the encoder a safe reference

An encoder reports counts, not degrees. To convert it into an angle, first choose what angle should mean zero. In this example, zero is the arm's safe lower position.

With the robot powered off, support the arm and place it at that reference mark. Then reset the encoder at initialization:

<Tabs items={['Java', 'Kotlin']}>
<Tab value="Java">

```java
arm = hardwareMap.get(DcMotor.class, "arm");
arm.setZeroPowerBehavior(DcMotor.ZeroPowerBehavior.BRAKE);
arm.setMode(DcMotor.RunMode.STOP_AND_RESET_ENCODER);
arm.setMode(DcMotor.RunMode.RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER);
```

</Tab>
<Tab value="Kotlin">

```kotlin

arm.mode = DcMotor.RunMode.RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER
```

</Tab>
</Tabs>

`STOP_AND_RESET_ENCODER` makes the current encoder reading zero. It does **not** move or home the arm. Only reset it when the arm is physically at the reference angle. `RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER` lets our code calculate power from the reading on every loop.

Now measure the encoder's scale. Move the arm slowly between two measured angles and record the change in encoder counts. For example, if it changes by 336 counts while the arm moves 60°, then:

```text
counts per degree = 336 counts ÷ 60 degrees = 5.6 counts/degree
```

Those numbers are an example measurement, not a motor specification. Use your own measured signed value. If the encoder count decreases while the arm angle increases, `COUNTS_PER_DEGREE` should be negative.

<Tabs items={['Java', 'Kotlin']}>
<Tab value="Java">

```java
private static final double COUNTS_PER_DEGREE = 5.6; // Replace with your measurement

double currentAngle = arm.getCurrentPosition() / COUNTS_PER_DEGREE;
```

</Tab>
<Tab value="Kotlin">


</Tab>

**Check this before adding PID:** manually move the arm through a known angle and compare the displayed angle with your protractor. If the sign is reversed or the scale is wrong, fix this conversion first.

## Step 2: Choose a target and calculate the error

Use the D-pad to choose three test positions: 0°, 30°, or 60°. Keep targets inside the safe mechanical range of your arm.

Before connecting motor power, send `targetAngle`, `currentAngle`, and `error` to telemetry. At the zero mark, telemetry should show a current angle close to 0°. When the arm is below a 60° target, the error should be positive. This tells you whether the sensor math agrees with the physical arm.

## Step 3: Add the P term, and test direction at low power

The proportional term is the error multiplied by `kP`:

<Tabs items={['Java', 'Kotlin']}>
<Tab value="Java">

```java
double kP = 0.003; // A cautious starting example; tune for your mechanism

double power = kP * error;
power = Math.max(-0.25, Math.min(0.25, power)); // Low commissioning limit
arm.setPower(power);
```

</Tab>
<Tab value="Kotlin">

```kotlin
val kP = 0.003 // A cautious starting example; tune for your mechanism

var power = kP * error
power = max(-0.25, min(0.25, power)) // Low commissioning limit
arm.power = power
```

</Tab>
</Tabs>

Here `kP` is motor-power per degree. With a 40° error and `kP = 0.003`, the requested power is 0.12. The ±0.25 limit is just a cautious starting limit; do not assume it is safe or strong enough for every mechanism.

Test with the arm supported and watch closely. If positive error makes the arm move away from the target, stop, correct the motor direction or encoder sign, and test again. Increase `kP` in small steps only after the direction is correct.

**What to look for:**

- It barely moves and never gets close: raise `kP` a little.
- It reaches the target slowly without crossing it: P-only control may be good enough for this test.
- It swings past the target and comes back: keep the P value that moves it reliably, then continue to Step 4.

Do not copy a gain from another robot. Changing the arm length, gearing, load, encoder scale, or loop timing changes the behavior.

## Step 4: Add D to slow an approaching arm

A fast-moving arm can pass the target even when the error is small. The derivative term measures the arm's speed and acts like a brake. We calculate it from the **measured angle**, so choosing a new target does not create an artificial speed spike.

<Tabs items={['Java', 'Kotlin']}>
<Tab value="Java">

```java
double degreesPerSecond = (currentAngle - previousAngle) / deltaTimeSeconds;
double dTerm = -kD * degreesPerSecond;
```

</Tab>
<Tab value="Kotlin">

```kotlin
val degreesPerSecond = (currentAngle - previousAngle) / deltaTimeSeconds
val dTerm = -kD * degreesPerSecond
```

</Tab>
</Tabs>

The minus sign makes the D term resist current motion. `deltaTimeSeconds` must be measured from a monotonic clock, such as `System.nanoTime()`, because loop timing can vary. The finished example below handles the first loop separately.

Start with `kD = 0`. After P moves the arm in the right direction, add a small D gain and change only that value at a time. If the arm slows smoothly near the target, D is helping. If it barely moves or jitters, reduce D; encoder noise can make derivative control noisy.

## Step 5: Add I only to fix a persistent small error

If the arm repeatedly stops a few degrees short because of friction or load, the integral term can accumulate that leftover error:

<Tabs items={['Java', 'Kotlin']}>
<Tab value="Java">

```java
integral += error * deltaTimeSeconds;
integral = Math.max(-MAX_INTEGRAL, Math.min(MAX_INTEGRAL, integral));
double iTerm = kI * integral;
```

</Tab>
<Tab value="Kotlin">

```kotlin
integral += error * deltaTimeSeconds
integral = max(-MAX_INTEGRAL, min(MAX_INTEGRAL, integral))
val iTerm = kI * integral
```

</Tab>
</Tabs>

The limit prevents the stored error from growing without bound while the arm is blocked or cannot reach its target. Keep `kI` at zero while you tune P and D. Add a very small value only if your measured tests show a steady offset. Too much I causes the arm to keep pushing after it reaches the target.

## Put the pieces together

This complete Java example keeps the controller small enough to follow. The starting gains are deliberately modest examples, not guaranteed tuning values. Begin with `KI` and `KD` set to zero; tune on the real robot using the steps above. Change one gain at a time and keep the arm supported.

<Tabs items={['Java', 'Kotlin']}>
<Tab value="Java">

```java
package org.firstinspires.ftc.teamcode;

import com.qualcomm.robotcore.eventloop.opmode.LinearOpMode;
import com.qualcomm.robotcore.eventloop.opmode.TeleOp;
import com.qualcomm.robotcore.hardware.DcMotor;

@TeleOp(name = "Guided PID Arm")
public class GuidedPidArm extends LinearOpMode {
    // Measure this on your robot; the sign matters.
    private static final double COUNTS_PER_DEGREE = 5.6;
    private static final double KP = 0.003;
    private static final double KI = 0.0;
    private static final double KD = 0.0;
    private static final double MAX_POWER = 0.25;

    private DcMotor arm;
    private final ArmPid pid = new ArmPid(KP, KI, KD);

    @Override
    public void runOpMode() {
        arm = hardwareMap.get(DcMotor.class, "arm");
        arm.setZeroPowerBehavior(DcMotor.ZeroPowerBehavior.BRAKE);
        arm.setMode(DcMotor.RunMode.STOP_AND_RESET_ENCODER);
        arm.setMode(DcMotor.RunMode.RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER);

        double targetAngle = 0.0;
        telemetry.addLine("Check that the arm is at its zero reference.");
        telemetry.addLine("Press START only when the arm area is clear.");
        telemetry.update();
        waitForStart();
        pid.reset();

        while (opModeIsActive()) {
            if (gamepad1.dpad_down) targetAngle = 0.0;
            if (gamepad1.dpad_left) targetAngle = 30.0;
            if (gamepad1.dpad_up) targetAngle = 60.0;
            if (gamepad1.b) break;

            double currentAngle = arm.getCurrentPosition() / COUNTS_PER_DEGREE;
            double output = pid.calculate(targetAngle, currentAngle);
            double power = Math.max(-MAX_POWER, Math.min(MAX_POWER, output));
            arm.setPower(power);

            telemetry.addData("Target (deg)", "%.1f", targetAngle);
            telemetry.addData("Measured (deg)", "%.1f", currentAngle);
            telemetry.addData("Error (deg)", "%.1f", targetAngle - currentAngle);
            telemetry.addData("Motor power", "%.2f", power);
            telemetry.addLine("Press B to stop the test.");
            telemetry.update();
        }

        arm.setPower(0.0);
    }

    private static class ArmPid {
        private static final double MAX_INTEGRAL = 300.0; // degree-seconds
        private final double kP;
        private final double kI;
        private final double kD;
        private double integral;
        private double previousAngle;
        private long previousTimeNanos;

        ArmPid(double kP, double kI, double kD) {
            this.kP = kP;
            this.kI = kI;
            this.kD = kD;
        }

        double calculate(double targetAngle, double currentAngle) {
            long now = System.nanoTime();
            double error = targetAngle - currentAngle;

            if (previousTimeNanos == 0L) {
                previousTimeNanos = now;
                previousAngle = currentAngle;
                return kP * error;
            }

            double dt = (now - previousTimeNanos) / 1_000_000_000.0;
            previousTimeNanos = now;
            if (dt <= 0.0 || dt > 0.1) {
                previousAngle = currentAngle;
                return kP * error + kI * integral;
            }

            integral += error * dt;
            integral = Math.max(-MAX_INTEGRAL, Math.min(MAX_INTEGRAL, integral));
            double degreesPerSecond = (currentAngle - previousAngle) / dt;
            previousAngle = currentAngle;

            return kP * error + kI * integral - kD * degreesPerSecond;
        }

        void reset() {
            integral = 0.0;
            previousAngle = 0.0;
            previousTimeNanos = 0L;
        }
    }
}
```

### Read the telemetry while tuning

1. Select `Guided PID Arm`, initialize, and confirm the displayed angle matches the real arm's zero position.
2. Press D-pad left for the 30° target. Watch the target, measured angle, error, and power together.
3. If the measured angle moves the wrong way, press B to stop and fix encoder or motor direction before continuing.
4. If it moves correctly but stops short or overshoots, adjust only `KP` first. Record each value and result.
5. Once P moves smoothly, add a little `KD` if it passes the target. Add `KI` only if repeated tests show a small persistent offset.
6. Test the full safe range and expected load before relying on the arm in autonomous or a match.

A build only checks that the code compiles. It does not prove the encoder scale, motor direction, gains, or mechanism limits are safe.

## If it behaves unexpectedly

| What you observe | Check this first |
| --- | --- |
| Angle changes the wrong way | Encoder sign and motor direction; stop testing until corrected. |
| Angle is the wrong size | Re-measure counts per degree and check the reference angle. |
| Arm moves but overshoots | Lower `KP`, then add a small `KD` after P works. |
| Arm chatters around the target | Reduce `KP` or `KD`; check for loose mechanics and noisy encoder readings. |
| Arm settles short | Verify gravity/load behavior; only then try a small `KI` or a gravity feedforward term. |
| Arm keeps pushing at a hard stop | Stop immediately; lower the target range and inspect the integral limit and mechanism. |
