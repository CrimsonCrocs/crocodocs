**System identification** estimates a mechanism's behaviour from recorded inputs and measurements. For motor feedforward, it turns test data into coefficients that predict the voltage needed for a requested motion. The result belongs to the tested mechanism, units, load, and operating range.

Start with [feedforward](./feedforward) and a trustworthy position or velocity measurement. This lesson explains a voltage model; it does not require installing the FRC WPILib runtime in an FTC project.

## Voltage model

For a simple mechanism without a significant gravity load, a useful model is:

$$
V = k_S\operatorname{sgn}(v) + k_Vv + k_Aa
$$

Here, $V$ is applied voltage, $v$ is velocity, and $a$ is acceleration. The static term approximates friction, the velocity term accounts for speed-dependent demand, and the acceleration term accounts for changing speed. Each term contributes **volts**.

| Coefficient | With velocity in m/s |
| --- | --- |
| $k_S$ | V |
| $k_V$ | V·s/m |
| $k_A$ | V·s²/m |

Radians or encoder ticks can also be used, but their gains have different units. A radians-based coefficient cannot be applied to a degrees-per-second target unchanged. Keep the encoder scale and transmission ratio with the fitted model.

A vertical slide needs a gravity term; an arm's gravity term varies with angle. Payload changes, flex, backlash, and asymmetric friction may require a different model or separate fitted cases. Near zero speed, the sign/friction model also needs a deliberate policy; a stationary mechanism does not automatically need the same command as one moving slowly.

## Test data

Log timestamps, measured position or velocity, commanded power, and bus voltage together. If estimating applied voltage as `motor power × bus voltage`, label it as an approximation: it is not a direct measurement of the motor terminals. Controller losses and saturation can affect the relationship.

Use a bounded test appropriate to the mechanism's travel, support, load, and current limits:

1. Apply a slowly increasing command to collect near-steady motion at several speeds. This helps separate friction from the velocity term.
2. Use short acceleration tests to make the acceleration term observable. Steady-speed data alone cannot identify $k_A$.
3. Repeat in both directions where the mechanism permits it. Check the sign conventions before combining those samples.
4. Keep separate runs for validation rather than fitting every recorded sample.

Estimate acceleration using the actual time interval between velocity samples. Differentiation magnifies noise; a smoother trace may also introduce delay. Check timestamps and measurement freshness before attributing a poor fit to the mechanism.

## Fitting the coefficients

Each recorded sample supplies a row of the form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{sgn}(v_i)&v_i&a_i\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}k_S\\k_V\\k_A\end{bmatrix}
\approx V_i
$$

With many samples, least squares chooses coefficients that minimise the summed squared voltage residuals. Use a numerical least-squares solver rather than explicitly inverting a matrix. The data must contain enough variation to distinguish the coefficients; many nearly identical samples do not solve that problem.

The following **invented data** illustrates the arithmetic for positive motion without gravity. It is not a robot test or a set of recommended gains.

| Velocity (m/s) | Acceleration (m/s²) | Applied voltage (V) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.5 | 0 | 1.3 |
| 1.0 | 0 | 2.3 |
| 1.0 | 2.0 | 3.1 |

The first two rows give $k_V=(2.3-1.3)/(1.0-0.5)=2.0$ V·s/m. Substituting either row gives $k_S=0.3$ V. The third row then gives $k_A=(3.1-0.3-2.0)/2.0=0.4$ V·s²/m.

At a desired 0.8 m/s and 1.5 m/s², this model predicts $0.3+2.0(0.8)+0.4(1.5)=2.5$ V. A new measured trial should test that prediction before the model is used to control motion.

## Validation

For the held-out runs, compare predicted voltage with the recorded applied-voltage estimate. Plot the residual against velocity, acceleration, direction, and time. A direction-dependent residual suggests a missing asymmetry; an error that grows with speed may indicate a scale or velocity-term problem.

Also test the resulting velocity response with feedback enabled at a bounded output. A good fit to one recording does not establish safe control over the full travel. Keep hard limits, timeouts, invalid-sensor handling, and the stop policy outside the model fit.

## FTC motor power

FTC `setPower()` takes a normalised command whose meaning depends on the motor mode. This voltage conversion belongs to a custom controller using **`RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER`**. In `RUN_USING_ENCODER` or `RUN_TO_POSITION`, power participates in the SDK's velocity/position behaviour instead; do not assume it is the same voltage command.

For the open-loop/custom-controller path, convert approximately using:

```text
requested volts = feedforward volts + feedback volts
requested power = requested volts / measured bus volts
```

Validate the voltage measurement against the controller's expected operating range before dividing. An invalid or stale measurement should disable the command rather than produce a large output. Apply the mechanism's travel limits and final power cap after combining the terms. Log both the requested and clipped output so saturation is visible.

For the illustrative 2.5 V request, the conversion gives about 0.208 at 12 V and 0.250 at 10 V. It cannot create voltage the battery and controller cannot supply. A clipped command means the requested model input was not delivered.

During control, feed the model the **desired** velocity and acceleration from a [motion profile](./motion-profiling). During identification, fit against the **measured** motion. Keep those two uses distinct, and refit after a meaningful change to gearing, payload, or mechanism geometry.
