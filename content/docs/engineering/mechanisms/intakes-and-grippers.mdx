title: Intakes and grippers
description: Design capture geometry, compliance, retention, and jam recovery around the objects your robot handles.
---

An **intake** brings an object into controlled contact with the robot. A **gripper** retains it for manipulation. Both need to work with the object's actual shape, surface, orientation, and permitted handling conditions.

## Capture area [#define-the-capture-region]

Specify the object set and the range of lateral offsets, approach angles, and initial orientations the mechanism should tolerate. That range is the **capture envelope**. If an intake only collects carefully placed objects, the driver must align the robot for every pickup.

Use guides, funnels, rollers, or shaped fingers to direct the object toward a repeatable retained position. Keep the path visible or provide reliable sensing.

## Active and passive intakes [#choose-active-or-passive-contact]

| Approach | Useful when | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roller or belt intake | Objects need to be drawn through a capture path | Compression, rejection, entanglement, and jams |
| Actuated claw | The object needs a defined pose or controlled release | Driver alignment, closing geometry, and load on the servo |
| Passive fingers or scoop | Geometry can retain the object without continuous actuation | Approach tolerance, release, and impact forces |

For passive intakes and claws, compare complete acquisition and release behavior; part count alone does not choose the best mechanism.

## Compliance [#use-compliance-deliberately]

**Compliance** is controlled deformation under load. A flexible roller or finger can accommodate object variation, increase contact, and absorb impact. Excessive deformation can also reduce positional consistency or let the object escape.

Roller compression is the interference between the unloaded roller geometry and the object path. Too little may not provide useful traction; too much raises load, damages objects, or stalls the mechanism. Tune spacing using adjustable mounts and representative objects.

Roller surface speed is $v = \pi Dn/60$. For an illustrative 50 mm roller at 500 RPM, it is about 1.31 m/s. The object's actual speed will differ because contact slips and deforms. A higher roller speed can increase rejection if the downstream space cannot control the object.

## Retention and release [#retain-and-release-predictably]

Check what happens when the robot brakes, turns, or raises the retained object. A grip that survives a stationary test may lose the object when inertia changes the load direction.

For a simplified friction grip with two opposing contacts, each pressing with normal force $N$, the available tangential force is roughly $2\mu N$. This assumes stable friction and suitable contact geometry. Use it to see which quantities matter, then test the actual object; compliant materials and irregular surfaces rarely match a single fixed coefficient.

Release should produce a repeatable pose and path. Make room for fingers to open without colliding with a scoring target or the robot. A passive retention feature can reduce holding current, but it must still permit the intended release.

## Jam recovery [#design-recovery-before-speed]

Provide a way to reverse rollers, reopen a claw, or return to an acquisition position. Avoid a pocket that traps an object where no actuator can reach it. Protect cables and fingers from rollers and pinch points.

Use a test grid with lateral offset on one axis and object angle on the other. Record captures, time, rejects, jams, and successful recoveries for each condition. Fix geometry that fails a whole region before trying to hide it with a longer timeout.

Compare the distribution of acquisition times and driver corrections as well as the average. A few long jams can account for much of the time spent collecting.
