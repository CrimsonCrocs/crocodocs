title: Turrets
description: Rotate a mechanism independently of the chassis while managing bearing loads, cable travel, reference position, and control error.

A **turret** rotates a mechanism in yaw, allowing it to aim or position independently of the drivetrain. That freedom adds a rotating support, transmission, position reference, and cable-management problem.

## Turret selection [#decide-whether-rotation-earns-its-cost]

A small rotating gripper can reduce alignment work without moving the whole robot. A base turret carrying slides or a shooter can offer a larger workspace, but it carries more mass and overturning load.

Compare the complete task with chassis-only alignment. Include acquisition and transfer geometry, settling time, driver workload, and the space swept by the mechanism.

## Bearing loads [#support-all-relevant-loads]

The rotating support may experience vertical load, radial load, and a moment from an offset mechanism. Choose a bearing arrangement rated for those loads. A low-friction turntable intended for vertical load is not automatically suitable for a long arm or sudden side impact.

For a simple static example, a 20 N load located 150 mm from the rotation axis produces a 3 N·m overturning moment. Moving the same load closer to the axis reduces that demand. The drive gear's torque rating does not establish the support bearing's moment capacity.

Possible arrangements include a central bearing, a suitable turntable bearing, or a deliberately designed perimeter support. Each needs retention and alignment. Check the actual bearing manufacturer's rating and installation method rather than choosing by visual similarity.

## Travel and cables [#define-travel-and-cable-behavior]

Decide whether the turret has limited or continuous travel. For limited rotation, route cables so the full allowed sweep cannot pull connectors, pinch wires, or wind them around a shaft. Include mechanism extension and chassis motion in that check.

Mechanical stops protect against overtravel, while software limits should stop normal motion before impact. For continuous travel, an electrical rotary interface may be needed. Its current, signal compatibility, and current-season legality require separate verification; ordinary wires do not permit indefinite rotation.

## Homing [#establish-a-reference]

An incremental encoder needs a known reference after startup. Choose a home sensor, an absolute-position sensor, or a repeatable manual reference procedure suited to the mechanism. Confirm the sensor's mounting rigidity and what happens if homing fails.

Account for the exact transmission ratio and backlash. Motor-shaft feedback can report motion while output slack is being taken up. Output sensing can expose that difference if the task needs it.

## Pointing accuracy [#check-pointing-error-where-it-matters]

A small angular error produces a larger lateral error at a distant target. For small angles in radians, lateral error is approximately $e = R\delta\theta$. At an illustrative 1.5 m distance, a 1-degree error is about $1.5(\pi/180) = 0.026$ m, or 26 mm.

That estimate excludes target motion, chassis localization error, projectile behavior, and mechanism flex. It helps allocate an angular tolerance; it does not establish aiming accuracy.

Test homing, reversals, travel endpoints, cable clearance, and loaded settling across the allowed rotation. Repeat after a typical impact or reassembly and check whether the zero reference and pointing remain repeatable.
