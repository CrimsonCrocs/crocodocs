**Material selection** matches a part's loads and environment to a material and manufacturing process. The best choice also fits the team's tools, time, budget, and replacement plan.

## Material properties [#distinguish-the-properties]

**Stiffness** controls elastic deflection. **Strength** limits loads before yielding or failure. **Toughness** describes resistance to fracture while absorbing energy. **Hardness** concerns local indentation or wear. These properties are related but are not interchangeable.

Geometry matters too. A deeper section can resist bending more efficiently than extra material near its center. For a simple rectangular section bending about its thickness direction, the second moment of area is $I = bh^3/12$. Doubling thickness $h$ multiplies this geometric quantity by eight while doubling mass, assuming the other dimensions and material remain unchanged. Holes, joints, load direction, and local failure still need checking.

## Material selection [#choose-from-likely-uses]

| Material | Common useful role | Limitation to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aluminum alloy | Frames, plates, brackets, and structural members | Alloy and temper, joint stiffness, bendability, fatigue |
| Steel | Shafts, wear surfaces, and compact highly loaded parts | Mass, corrosion, machinability, and exact grade |
| Polycarbonate | Impact-tolerant guards and shaped guides | Flex, scratching, creep, and fabrication method |
| Acetal or HDPE | Low-friction guides, spacers, and machined plastic parts | Stiffness, creep, retention, and dimensional change |
| Printed thermoplastic | Custom guides, fixtures, adapters, and cable relief | Layer direction, temperature, fit, and repeatability |
| Flexible TPU | Compliant fingers, rollers, and contact surfaces | Grip, wear, deformation, and printing setup |
| Carbon-fiber composite | Selected lightweight structural parts | Directional properties, edge damage, joining, and dust handling |

Use supplier data for numerical properties; generic material names do not specify a rated part.

## Stock specifications [#specify-the-actual-stock]

"Aluminum" is incomplete purchasing information. Record alloy, temper, thickness, form, and tolerances where relevant. A bendable sheet alloy and a heat-treated structural plate can behave differently during forming even if both look similar.

Use supplier data to decide a drawing's material specification. Do not transfer a property table from one alloy or temper to another.

For polymers, distinguish solid stock from a printed part. A filament supplier's material properties do not establish the strength of your layer orientation, infill, holes, and screw joints. Test the manufactured part under the load direction it will experience.

## Mass reduction [#reduce-mass-without-losing-the-load-path]

Removing material from a plate can reduce mass, but every cutout changes stiffness and stress distribution. Preserve support around bearings, fasteners, and high-load interfaces. Avoid sharp internal corners and narrow bridges unless their load has been assessed.

A decorative pattern is not evidence of an optimized structure. Compare loaded deflection and failure behavior before and after a mass reduction.

## Part testing [#validate-a-candidate-part]

Make a representative sample with the actual process and joints. Check fit first, then introduce a safe load gradually. Test repeated cycles, impact where relevant, and time under load when creep matters. Keep people clear and contain a possible failure.

Record stock or filament identity, dimensions, revision, process, load, and result. New teams can begin with established kit members and use custom materials for small low-risk interfaces. Experienced teams should retain a known working replacement until a new material has passed equivalent tests.
