A **shaft** transfers torque; a **bearing** supports motion while limiting unwanted movement. Reliable rotation requires both a way to transmit torque and a way to carry the forces applied to the assembly.

## Drive and support [#separate-drive-from-support]

A motor turns a wheel, but the wheel also carries robot weight and collision loads. Mounting the wheel far from the gearbox adds a bending moment to its output shaft. External bearings can carry that load while a transmission carries torque.

For a simplified overhung load, the bending moment at the support is $M = Fd$, where $F$ is the perpendicular force and $d$ its distance from the support. An illustrative 40 N load at 60 mm produces $2.4$ N·m; moving it to 20 mm reduces that moment to $0.8$ N·m. This is a geometry comparison, not a shaft load rating.

![An overhung wheel loads a single support with a bending moment; a wheel between two supports shares its load through both sides.](/guides/engineering/shaft-support.svg)

Use the actual gearbox's radial, axial, and torque ratings. Overhung loading and shock loads affect gearbox capacity.

## Live and dead axles [#live-axle-or-dead-axle]

In a **live axle**, the shaft rotates and the mechanism is locked to it through a hub, key, hex profile, or other torque connection. Bearings support the rotating shaft in the frame.

In a **dead axle**, the axle stays fixed while the wheel or mechanism rotates around it on bearings. A sprocket, pulley, or gear must drive the rotating component itself. The fixed axle can also connect structural plates when designed for that role.

## Shaft interfaces [#match-every-interface]

Check shaft profile and size, bearing inner diameter, bearing outer diameter, housing fit, and hub bore. A nominally round, hexagonal, D-shaped, or REX shaft needs compatible hardware; a near fit is not adequate torque transmission.

Ball bearings have an **inner race** and **outer race** separated by rolling elements. Support and retain the correct races. When installing a bearing with an interference fit, apply installation force to the race being fitted, following the bearing supplier's instructions; avoid driving that force through the rolling elements.

Align supports from common datums. Tightening a pair of plates around a crooked shaft can create friction that looks like a weak motor. Check shaft rotation as each structural joint is tightened.

## Axial retention [#stop-axial-motion-without-squeezing-the-bearing]

**Axial retention** prevents a shaft sliding along its length. Use compatible collars, retaining rings, shoulders, or end screws to constrain both directions. Retain the bearings in their housings too.

Place spacers and shims so a rotating shaft's retention stack bears on the intended inner race without rubbing the stationary outer race or seal. Do not tighten a stack until it binds just to eliminate all visible clearance.

## Friction checks [#diagnose-friction-in-stages]

Disconnect power. Where the design permits, remove the belt or chain and compare the supported shaft alone, then add each hub, spacer, and transmission. A tight spot once per revolution suggests runout, a bent part, or an eccentric mount; resistance that appears only after tightening suggests alignment or an incorrect clamping stack.

Mark the position of collars and hubs, run a controlled test, and recheck. Inspect shaft migration, bearing movement, dust from rubbing parts, and loose torque connections. Fix those causes before increasing motor command or retuning control gains.
