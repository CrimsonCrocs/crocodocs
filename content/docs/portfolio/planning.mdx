Use your notebook, test logs, task board, and outreach records to choose what goes in the portfolio. Select the work you can explain and support within the page limit.

## Collecting evidence

After a build session or outreach activity, record the date, the problem or objective, what you did, and the result. Link the original measurement sheet, attendance record, or other supporting material.

For a robot change, save a photo of the earlier version before dismantling it. For a lesson from a mentor, record the advice and the student work it changed. For a workshop, record the audience and follow-up while the details are available.

Keep using your team's shared folders and planning tools. A short table can help you find the records when writing:

| Date | Work | Decision or result | Supporting record | Page owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Session date | Subsystem or project | What changed and why | Photo, test sheet, drawing, or activity log | Student responsible for explaining it |

## First draft

Draft one completed investigation first. Explain the objective, problem, change, test, and result. Add a labelled photo or diagram and a comparison that supports the conclusion.

Ask a teammate who did not do the work to read it and explain why the design changed. If they can only name the new parts, explain the decision more clearly.

Draft a member-learning example and an outreach project the same way. You can begin those pages while the robot and other projects are still in progress.

## Page plan

BIOBUZZ A201 allows one cover and up to fifteen content pages. The plan below is **one possible allocation**, not a prescribed order or a requirement to fill every page.

| Content page | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Team overview, current-season highlights, and references to supporting pages |
| 2 | Member development and applied learning |
| 3 | Finances, recruitment, leadership handover, and progress tracking |
| 4 | STEM partnerships and what the team learned from them |
| 5 | Outreach objectives, audience, and project plan |
| 6 | Outreach results, recruitment, and follow-up |
| 7 | Game requirements and the team's design priorities |
| 8 | Whole-robot overview and subsystem integration |
| 9 | Mechanical concepts and the chosen design |
| 10 | A significant iteration and its test results |
| 11 | A calculation that informed a decision and how it was checked |
| 12 | Control system, sensors, and software behavior |
| 13 | Control testing, reliability, and recovery |
| 14 | Maintenance, failure analysis, and whole-robot testing |
| 15 | The most useful remaining investigation or lesson from the season |

Change the balance to match your team's evidence. A single thorough mechanism study can cover process, calculations, iteration, and risk in fewer pages. Avoid dividing one explanation across distant pages just to match an award list.

## Choosing material

Check what each item tells the reader. Another photograph of the same demo may add little. A record of participants later joining FIRST answers a different question: what happened after the demo?

Keep technical detail that explains a decision. Store raw logs, complete code, meeting notes, and extra photos in the team's working records, and include enough in the portfolio to support its conclusions.

## Ownership and revisions

Assign a student to gather and check each topic. Once contributors have checked their claims, have one editor check terminology, dates, units, and layout across the document.

Keep a record of which robot version and date each page describes. After an event, replace outdated results and add the decisions that followed. A saved PDF version makes it easier to know what was submitted and to interpret later feedback.
