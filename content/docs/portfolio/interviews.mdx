Practice explaining your work and answering questions about it. Know where to find the relevant portfolio page so you can discuss the decision, setback, or result without searching the whole document.

## Initial interview

Under BIOBUZZ TU04 A205–A207, teams receive equal initial-interview time of at least ten minutes. A team may use the first five minutes for an uninterrupted prepared presentation, then discuss its work with judges. Check the format and arrangements communicated for your event.

Choose examples from the robot, team development, and outreach. Have students explain work they know and rehearse the handovers between speakers. Leave time for questions.

A simple structure is a short team introduction, a design investigation, a learning or sustainability example, and an outreach result. Adjust the time to your actual evidence rather than giving every topic an equal allocation.

## Answering questions

Begin with the answer, then explain the example and its evidence. If asked why you changed an intake, identify the failure and design decision before describing every prototype in the season.

Practice identifying the difference between a fact you measured, an estimate, and something the team still needs to test. If you do not know a detail, say so and let the teammate who did the work answer.

The 2026–27 interview process begins Q&A with one MCI and one Team Attributes question selected by the Judge Advisor. Use the published question bank to practice, but expect other questions too.

## Rehearsal

Ask a teammate or mentor to choose questions without telling presenters in advance. Use prompts about the actual work, such as:

- What evidence persuaded you to change this design?
- What did a new member learn, and what could they do independently afterward?
- Which outreach result can you confirm, and how?
- What happens when this sensor fails?
- What will keep the team operating when current leaders leave?

Afterward, note any confusing terms, unsupported answers, or pages that were hard to find. Fix those problems and practice with different questions.

## Pit conversations

Keep the portfolio available and know where relevant prototypes, drawings, and test records are. A failed part can help explain an iteration when students can connect it to the later decision.

During a follow-up, answer the question asked and offer the relevant demonstration. Judges can examine additional material in the pit, but do not take it back for deliberation. Put the essential explanation in the submitted portfolio.

Arrange demonstrations so they are ready and do not create delay. The interview should remain understandable if a robot function cannot be demonstrated at that moment.

## Feedback

Review feedback alongside the submitted portfolio and the team's recollection of the discussion. BIOBUZZ A212 says initial-interview feedback reflects the panel's initial impression; it is not the deliberation record or a complete explanation of the award outcome.

Use feedback to decide which explanations or answers need work. One event's outcome does not tell you what every judge prefers or which layout will win an award.
