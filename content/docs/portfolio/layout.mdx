Use short headings and keep related explanations, pictures, and results together. A judge should be able to find a topic and see what the page is trying to explain before reading it closely.

## Page structure

Decide what the page needs to explain: an intake change, programmer training, or the result of a school partnership. Choose and arrange the material around that subject.

A mechanism page might put the earlier and revised designs beside a results table, followed by the decision and remaining problem. An outreach page might need a timeline and a project example. Use consistent type and margins, but arrange each page around its content.

Make the main comparison easy to find. Keep secondary labels and details quieter than the heading and main result.

## Headings

Use short topic names such as **Intake testing**, **Member training**, **Budget**, or **Autonomous results**. Describe the actual subject rather than making a slogan out of the conclusion.

An opening overview can list major achievements with page references. Check that each referenced page supports the claim.

## Explanations

Use the space to explain the team's decisions. Replace a long general definition of backspin with the relevant observation, the design response, and the test that followed. Define enough physics for the reader to understand why the change could help.

Compare these **illustrative drafts**:

> We made our intake better and more reliable.

> Samples caught on the entrance plate. We widened the opening and repeated the same pickup test. The revised intake completed eighteen of twenty trials, compared with twelve of twenty before the change.

The second version names the problem, change, and result. Add the test conditions and design version in the final portfolio, and replace the example numbers with your records.

## Type and contrast

BIOBUZZ A201's readability guidance warns against text below 10 pt and low contrast. Use that as a warning boundary, not a target to shrink every paragraph to. Check a printed page at its intended size and a PDF at ordinary reading zoom.

Use a readable body font and reserve display lettering for short headings. Keep text off busy photos, leave space between unrelated blocks, and use color consistently. A chart should remain understandable in grayscale through its labels or line styles.

The case-study portfolios have distinct visual identities, but some pages are dense. Learn from their organization without inheriting small type or difficult color combinations.

## Figures and charts

Label the component or change each figure explains. Use a close view when the detail would be hard to see in a full-robot image.

On a chart, identify the measured quantity, units, test conditions, and trial count. Clearly distinguish a target, prediction, and observed result. Crop screenshots to the relevant region while preserving the context needed to interpret them.

Use diagrams and photos your team owns or has permission to publish. Credit outside designs and research. Studying another team's portfolio does not transfer ownership of its artwork or writing.

## Tools

Canva, Figma, presentation software, and document editors can all produce a usable PDF. Choose a tool contributors can access and revise reliably, with the correct page size and a legible export.

Set shared styles for headings, body text, labels, and figures. Draft the text before decorating the pages, and check that the exported PDF still looks right.

## Sources and AI

Keep compact source credits for borrowed concepts, diagrams, and outside assistance. BIOBUZZ A201 permits AI and research aids with respect for intellectual property and a footnote or endnote credit.

Check generated feedback against the current manual and your records. Do not add a suggested achievement or result unless the team actually did it. Students should understand and be able to explain the final text.
