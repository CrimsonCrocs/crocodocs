Blu Cru, team 6417, won the **Goodall Division Inspire Award at the 2026 FIRST Championship**. Its DECODE portfolio shows how the team applied what it learned from mentors and explains design risks, software behavior, and manual recovery.

## The reference copy

The supplied PDF has one cover and fifteen content pages. It is a scanned, redacted reference copy: some student, mentor, financial, and partner details are intentionally withheld. This analysis uses the visible content and does not infer the missing details.

| Pages | What to examine |

| 2–4 | Roles, skill development, season planning, and organizational risks |
| 5–8 | Outreach and professional learning |
| 9–13 | Robot versions, requirements, design analysis, and mechanisms |
| 14–15 | Autonomous behavior, sensing, TeleOp, and recovery |

## Learning and design

Page 3 begins with skill gaps: manufacturing, vision, design analysis, and related technical work. It connects those gaps to mentors, older students, alumni, and offseason projects. Page 8 describes reviews and areas of guidance rather than relying only on mentor portraits.

Page 10 shows the team using that learning in requirements, prototypes, CAD, simulations, and failure analysis. Details such as corner relief and assembly checks show how the students applied manufacturing skills.

Follow one skill from the need for training through to the student's completed work and its review. Say what the mentor taught and what the student did.

## Requirements

Page 10 translates its game analysis into design priorities. It connects resistance to pushing with drivetrain choices, the arrangement of game pieces with intake width, and time spent shooting with the transfer and launcher architecture.

The page explains why each requirement led to a design choice. In your portfolio, include the alternatives and the test that shows whether the chosen design met the requirement.

## Mechanism development

Pages 11–13 combine physical views with analysis of the drivetrain, intake, transfer, turret, and launchers. Page 12 follows several transfer-linkage versions and includes a force explanation for counterspringing. Page 13 uses a launcher calculation to discuss an operating range.

The calculations address design questions. Include your inputs, assumptions, units, and the decision you made from the result. Check the design physically before making a reliability claim.

The portfolio also describes a mechanical way of securing the turret after a failure. The reader can identify both the risk and the response.

## Driver recovery

Page 15 uses a state diagram to explain the driver cycle and shows paths for an outtake failsafe and manual control. The adjacent aiming explanation describes a rough estimate from robot position followed by camera-based adjustment. It also discusses what happens when normal automation needs operator intervention.

The diagram lets a reader follow an input through the robot's actions and see what happens after a mistake. You can explain that behavior without printing the entire implementation.

Explain how recovery was tested and what feedback confirms the robot is ready to resume. Keep commands, sensor events, and manual overrides distinct in a diagram.

## Reading limits

An award ribbon at the top of each page marks relevant categories. If you use one, highlight only the categories the page supports.

Some pages put dense diagrams and small text on saturated backgrounds. Check a printout and grayscale export before using similar colors. Treat performance claims without documented test conditions as team-reported outcomes, not verified guarantees.

## Applying it

Write up one lesson that changed a design. For the software, draw the normal cycle and add the failure and recovery paths. Use your records to explain what worked, and preserve any withheld personal details.
