An outreach page should explain who you intended to help, what you did, and what happened afterward. Use dates, audience records, participants' work, and follow-up to support the results.

## Objectives

Decide what you want the project to achieve. Introducing families to FIRST, helping a new FLL team get started, and teaching programming need different measures of success.

Explain why you chose the audience and activity. Describe how you handled any limits on travel, cost, language, or equipment. For Reach, explain how the project helped grow FIRST.

## Project records

Keep a short record for each significant project:

| Part | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Objective | The audience and change the team intended |
| Contribution | Work completed by your students, partners, and other teams |
| Delivery | Dates, frequency, format, and material provided |
| Results | Attendance, work completed, recruitment, or another relevant outcome |
| Follow-up | Continued support, feedback, and the next action actually agreed |

For recurring workshops, explain how feedback changed later sessions. Record what participants could do afterward if you checked their work. Keep attendance separate from claims about learning or recruitment.

## FIRST terminology

FIRST's September 2026 Outreach Terms and Definitions gives specific meanings to **Started**, **Sustained**, **Mentored**, **Published Resources**, **Hosted**, **Supported**, **Reached**, and **Advocated**. Check the full definition before using one as a claim.

Mentoring involves a consistent relationship and agreement from the team receiving help. Publishing a guide or answering one question is valuable support, but does not establish that relationship. Starting a team has formation or funding conditions, agreement, first-season support, and official participation conditions. Keep the records needed to substantiate the particular term.

When a definition does not fit, name what you did: delivered parts, ran two training sessions, reviewed CAD, or helped with event setup.

## Counting people

Keep the measure beside the number. Workshop participants, unique online viewers, video views, resource downloads, volunteers recruited, and teams receiving ongoing help are different quantities.

**Illustrative example:** twenty-four students attend three sessions, with the same students returning each time. That is twenty-four individual participants and seventy-two session attendances. Calling it seventy-two different students would overstate the audience.

For a public event, count the people who observed or interacted with the team using a defensible method. Total attendance at a large venue does not establish attendance at your exhibit. If you estimate, explain how and use a conservative figure.

Keep online figures separate unless you have a valid way to identify overlap. A person may watch several videos, follow multiple accounts, and attend an event. Adding those totals together does not produce a verified unique audience.

## Recruitment and lasting results

Reach's criteria include successful recruitment of new FIRST participants. Record the path from the activity to participation: who joined, in which role or program, what your team contributed, and what confirms that result. Express planned recruitment as a plan until it happens.

For published resources, show how people used them through feedback, team examples, or workshop records. Date any usage figures and explain what they count. Downloads do not establish that everyone used the resource.

## Case study or event list

Give more space to projects whose purpose, ongoing work, and results you can explain. Use a timeline for other activities instead of repeating a similar paragraph for every event.

Both illustrate documentation choices; their reported totals remain their own claims.
