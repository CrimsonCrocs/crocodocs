Show how members learn, how the team pays for its work, and how new people take over when older members leave. Use examples from the season to explain the plans your team follows.

## Member learning

Choose a skill a student learned and show what they could do afterward. That might be a part they designed, code they wrote, or a workshop they led.

For CAD, the steps might be guided practice, a prototype, an independently designed part, and a design review. Use steps that suit your members and show which ones they have completed.

[Seattle Solvers](/docs/portfolio/case-studies/seattle-solvers) shows training followed by growing responsibility. Your team can use the same idea with its own size, schedule, and learning goals.

## STEM partnerships

Explain why you contacted a professional, what they taught you, and how students used the advice. Put a drawing or test result beside the explanation so readers can see the connection.

A sponsor may provide money, mentoring, or both. Explain each contribution and credit it accurately. A useful professional relationship does not need to involve sponsorship.

## Finances

Show where the money comes from, the main expenses, and how you handle a shortfall. Include the currency and dates. Make clear whether an amount is received, promised, spent, or budgeted.

Explain a decision the budget helped you make, such as changing travel plans, choosing how to manufacture a part, or running a fundraiser. Leave private account details and personal financial information out of the portfolio.

## Recruitment and handover

Think about which skills and jobs could be lost when members graduate. Show who is learning each job, how they practise, and what they can already do independently.

If you run a feeder program, explain where participants go afterward and what help they continue to receive. Separate what has happened from what you plan to do.

## Risks and progress

For Sustain, explain your plan and how you check whether it is working. Leadership training and preparation for problems can strengthen that explanation.

| Problem | What the team is doing | How you can check progress |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only one person understands autonomous | Pair programming and shared setup notes | Another student can set up and run a test |
| The manufacturing lead is graduating | Supervised practice for a successor | The new lead can prepare and check a part independently |
| Event costs are uncertain | A budget, fundraising plan, and spending reviews | Compare money received with expected costs |
| Build deadlines are slipping | Review tasks and adjust the plan | Check completed work against the revised dates |

Use your team's own examples. Explain what you changed when a progress review showed the plan was not working.
