Show what members learned, how they used it, and how the team prepares for future seasons. Use training records, completed work, budgets, and handover plans to explain what the team does.

## Member learning

Start with a skill the team needed. Explain how a student learned it, used it, and checked their progress. Include the resulting design, reviewed program, or independently led session alongside their role.

An example progression might be guided CAD practice, a small prototype, an independently designed component, and a design review. Use milestones appropriate to your members and show which ones actually happened.

The [Seattle Solvers study](/docs/portfolio/case-studies/seattle-solvers) examines a documented training sequence and increasing responsibility. You do not need their team size or the same training duration to explain your own members' progress.

## STEM partnerships

For each important relationship, explain how the connection began, why the team sought that person's expertise, what advice they gave, and how students used it. An annotated drawing or test change can connect a mentor's contribution to an engineering result.

Separate a sponsor's financial support from their mentoring role. A sponsor can contribute both, but a company logo alone does not demonstrate a learning relationship. Equally, a useful professional connection can exist without a sponsorship payment.

Say which work students completed and what guidance they received. Credit both accurately.

## Finances

Present the resources needed to operate, the sources of income, the main expenses, and how the team responds to shortfalls. Label currency, period, and whether money is received, pledged, spent, or budgeted.

Explain a decision made using the budget, such as adjusting travel, choosing a manufacturing method, or changing how you raised money. Date the figures and state what they cover.

Protect private account information, contact details, and personal financial circumstances. Summarize the operational facts needed to explain the plan.

## Recruitment and handover

Identify the knowledge or responsibilities at risk when members leave. Show who is learning each role, what documentation or practice supports them, and how you know they can perform the work.

For a feeder program, explain where participants went afterward and what support continued. Label future plans separately from completed work.

## Risks and progress

Sustain addresses planning and tracking progress, with leadership development and risk management as additional supporting criteria. A short table can connect those concerns to actual actions:

| Risk or constraint | Response | Evidence of progress |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One person understands autonomous | Pair programming and shared setup notes | Another student can configure and run a test |
| Graduating manufacturing lead | Supervised practice and a named successor | Successor independently prepares a checked part |
| Uncertain event costs | A dated budget, fundraising plan, and spending reviews | Received funds and revised projections |
| Missed build milestones | Review tasks and reduce or reorder scope | Updated plan and completed milestones |

Replace these examples with your team's risks and responses. Explain what you learned when reviewing progress and how that changed the plan.
